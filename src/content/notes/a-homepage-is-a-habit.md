I used to think a homepage needed an occasion. Finish a project, write an essay, have something presentable to put on the table. That makes every visit to your own website feel like preparing for company.

I'd rather have a place I can leave a book open.

## Make the next update small

A useful update can be one sentence about what you're working on. It can be a link with a reason to visit it, or a correction to an old note. The size matters less than making a decision someone else can understand.

My [now page](/now/) gives those small changes somewhere to go. If a project is paused, I can say so. If a book is still on the desk, it doesn't need a fresh opinion every week. A dated page makes room for that kind of honesty.

I keep a short list of possible updates in [a pocket notebook](/collections/a-pocket-notebook/). When I return to the site, I don't have to invent a subject. I just ask which scrap still seems worth keeping.

## Leave a way through

The other habit is checking paths. Does an old note point toward a related project? Can someone get back to the archive without using the browser's back button? Does a link still lead to the thing I described?

These are quiet jobs, but they change the experience of visiting. A small site can feel generous when its owner has thought about the next click.

## A routine I can keep

Open the homepage. Read the first paragraph aloud. Follow one link. Make one useful change, or leave everything as it is.

That's the whole routine. Some days it leads to [a longer note](/notes/the-useful-kind-of-unfinished/). Some days I fix a sentence and go outside. Either way, returning is easier when the site doesn't demand a performance.
