The short route is good when I need to get somewhere. The long route is good when I've spent too much of the day moving from one small rectangle to another.

I don't need a destination worthy of a photograph. A turn I usually skip is enough.

## Choose one thing to notice

Trying to notice everything can become another task. Instead, I give the walk one question. Where does the afternoon shade fall? Which windows have plants? What changes when the pavement meets the grass?

The question makes familiar streets legible in a different way. It also gives me a reason to stop that doesn't involve checking a screen. I can look at one patch of ground for a little longer than seems necessary.

This is the thought behind [my pocket field guide](/projects/pocket-field-guide/). Its pages begin with observations, not identifications. “A leaf with a ragged edge” is a useful starting point. I can look up a name later, and keep the uncertainty attached to the note.

## Bring back a question

I try to return with one question instead of a report. A question has somewhere to go: a book, a conversation, a search, another walk. It doesn't ask me to turn an ordinary afternoon into an impressive story.

[Public libraries](/collections/public-libraries/) are good places for that next step. A shelf can offer a neighboring subject that would never have occurred to me as a search term. Sometimes the question changes before I find an answer.

## Leave the walk alone

Not every outing needs notes. Some days the useful part is simply being away from the desk. I keep the notebook optional, and I don't measure how much I brought home.

When something does stay with me, I put it among [the things I'm collecting](/collections/). A small record is enough to remind me where a thought began. The rest of the walk can belong to the walk.
