Some books make me reach for another book. This one makes me want to put the book down for a while and pay better attention to what is outside.

I keep Robin Wall Kimmerer's _Braiding Sweetgrass_ close to [my reading notes](/projects/a-slower-reading-list/). I'm reading it a little at a time, leaving space between essays rather than trying to make steady progress through a stack.

## A way of reading

My notes begin with questions, not a summary of each chapter. What have I learned to notice? What am I receiving without thinking about it? What would it mean to respond with care in an ordinary part of my day?

Those questions are my reading response. They don't stand in for the book or for the knowledge it draws on. When I want to revisit an argument or a story, I return to the pages themselves and look at the surrounding context.

I use a scrap of paper as a bookmark and write page numbers beside anything I want to find again. That keeps the notes small and helps me avoid turning reading into a transcription exercise.

## Carry one question outside

After an essay, I sometimes choose a single question to carry on a walk. There is no need to force a connection. Often I just find myself looking longer at something I'd usually pass.

That attention is part of the reason I'm working on [a pocket field guide](/projects/pocket-field-guide/). I want to leave room for what I don't know before I hurry toward a name.

## Finding your own copy

I'd start with a library catalog or ask a bookseller about the available editions. [Public libraries](/collections/public-libraries/) also give a book neighbors: other work by the author, related subjects, and reading suggestions from people who know their shelves.

My note here is an invitation to read, slowly if that suits you. I'm leaving the argument and the stories where they belong, in the book.
